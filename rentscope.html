Where it started

The idea grew out of my years working with rental property data at a real estate analytics firm.

I analysed client and property data across rent management systems, resolved discrepancies and QA-checked the reports built from it. Working with that data makes one thing obvious: a price on its own tells a renter very little.

02

The problem

  • 01
    No context

    Listings show a price, but not how it compares with similar homes nearby. Renters can’t easily tell a fair price from an inflated one.

  • 02
    Hidden monthly costs

    Utilities, water storage and the commute change what a home really costs each month.

  • 03
    Local essentials buried

    Water tanks, security and bus times sit in the description, if they’re mentioned at all.

03

Who it’s for

Renters in and around Kingston 6, many of them a bus ride from UWI Mona, comparing a handful of homes on a budget.

Design goals

  • 01
    Put every price in context

    Show where each home sits against its neighbourhood before anyone calls.

  • 02
    Let renters judge for themselves

    A chart of similar homes, not a “good deal” badge.

  • 03
    Compare the real monthly cost

    Rent plus utilities plus bus fares.

  • 04
    Treat local details as data

    Water storage, security and bus times as proper fields.

04

How it works

From a search to a shortlist you can defend.

  1. Step 01

    Search an area

    Start with an area, like Kingston 6.

  2. Step 02

    Filter by what matters

    Budget, bedrooms, water storage and bus time to UWI.

  3. Step 03

    Scan against the median

    The map is shaded by median rent, and every listing shows its difference.

  4. Step 04

    Open a home

    A fair-price chart and real travel times beside the photos.

  5. Step 05

    Compare up to three

    Side by side on total monthly cost, with the best value named.

  6. Step 06

    Read the rent report

    How the area is moving, neighbourhood by neighbourhood.

07

Key design decisions

Price in context

Every listing shows where it sits against its neighbourhood’s median, right in the results list.

A chart instead of a claim

The fair-price check places a home on a histogram of similar homes nearby, so renters can judge for themselves.

The real monthly cost

Comparisons lead with rent plus utilities plus bus fares, not rent alone.

Local details are first-class

Water tanks, security and bus times to UWI are proper fields, not buried in a description.

Lead with the answer

Compare and the rent report open with a sentence that states the finding, then show the numbers behind it.

Label estimates as estimates

Utilities and bus costs are marked as estimates, so the total never pretends to be exact.

08

Visual language

Warm and editorial.

A serif for headings and prices gives it the feel of a well-made property guide. The terracotta shades double as the rent scale on the map, so the brand colour carries data. Sage appears only for green space.

What I’d test next

  • The test

    Talk to renters about how they judge a price today, and test whether the fair-price chart changes which homes they shortlist.

  • What I’d watch

    Which homes make the shortlist with and without the chart, and whether people can explain why.

  • Open questions

    Where would reliable rent data come from, and how often would it update? How many similar homes does the chart need before it’s trustworthy?
